Dermatophytosis

Characteristics of dermatophytes

  1. They belong to the imperfect fungi. The “perfect” state, i.e., the sexual phase, has been demonstrated in many dermatophytes, causing them to be transferred to the class Ascomycetes (5, 24).

  2. They are well-adapted parasites, although many of them survive for prolonged periods in soil. Based on host preference, fungi associated with animals are called zoophilic, and those associated with humans are anthropophilic (5, 24).

  3. Infection by a dermatophyte can lead to a state of hypersensitivity (5).

  4. Secondary bacterial invasion is common (5).

  5. They are capable of hydrolyzing keratin (5, 24).

  6. They are more frequent in housed animals than in grazing animals (5).

  7. In domestic animals there is no difference between the lesions produced by different dermatophytes (5).

  8. Dermatomycoses can be transmitted from animal to animal or from animal to human (5).

The most important genera of dermatophytes are Microsporum and Trichophyton (1, 5, 24).

Some differences between these two genera (5):

Genus Fluorescence Macroconidia Infection type
Microsporum Some species fluorescent Macroconidia frequently present; spinulate Endothrix
Trichophyton No fluorescence Few macroconidia; thin, non-spinulate wall Endothrix and ectothrix

PATHOGENESIS OF DERMATOPHYTOSIS

According to Bofill et al. (1996), the most favorable conditions for germination, growth and multiplication of dermatophyte spores occur in the hair follicle between the two root sheaths of the hair.

Fungal spores are protected in skin crevices and hair follicles. After germinating, the fungal hyphae grow within the hair shaft (endothrix).

Spores that reach skin abrasions germinate and develop on the skin surface beneath the layer of keratinized cells, from where they can also reach the hair follicles.

According to experimental inoculation studies carried out with T. verrucosum, the pathogenesis of the disease can be considered in 4 phases:

Incubation — during this period, generally between 7 and 17 days after inoculation, there is rapid invasion of the stratum corneum and the proximal superficial portion of the hair follicle, with long vegetative hyphae disseminated in these spaces. In the blood vessels of the dermis, numerous mononuclear cells can be seen, appearing in response to the presence of the pathogen.

Maturation — also called the dissemination phase, begins from 14–17 days after inoculation. During this stage, the fungus progressively invades the keratinized outer portion of the root sheath of the hair follicle, and primary formation of arthrospores (ectothrix) occurs at the pilosebaceous duct. By approximately 21 days, arthrospore proliferation is evident in the hair follicle lumen and the softer keratinized portion of the root sheath. By 28 days, the cuticle is penetrated, the cortex of the actively growing hair is invaded, and endothrix arthrospore formation can be seen. Hyphae can be observed in the pilosebaceous duct at this stage. Between 28–35 days, hyphae can be seen in the keratohyaline zone of the involuting follicles; fragmentation of the hair in the upper portions is significant at this time.

In the first animals this occurs between 28–49 days, while in calves it occurs approximately one week later. Capillary vasodilation of the dermis produces a serous exudate accompanied by numerous PMN cells that infiltrate the stratum corneum. Masses of PMN cells together with the exudate, infiltrated into the epidermis with acanthosis and parakeratosis, form the typical crusts. The exudate invades the hair follicles, forming microabscesses whose rupture occurs in the surrounding dermis. In the capillary bed of the mid-dermis, a lymphocytic perivascular infiltrate is frequently observed. Hair fragments surrounded by masses of arthrospores lie in the hyperkeratinized region of the cortex.

Inflammatory climax — the inflammatory response is more acute in adult animals than in young ones.

Regression — characterized by the birth and development of new hairs in the healed follicle. This period begins between 49–63 days after inoculation in animals of all ages, but it is generally earlier in adult animals. During this phase, fungi may still be observed in some histological sections; however, cultures of skin scrapings are negative. In the exudates, hyphae in a degenerative state can be seen. In the microabscess areas, the healing process begins, with granular fibrous tissue infiltrating. In the perivascular zones of the dermis, lymphocytic eosinophilic infiltration may be observed.

BOVINE TRICHOPHYTOSIS

Cutaneous mycosis affecting bovines of all ages. Disease normally of enzootic presentation. Other species that may be affected: sheep, pigs, horses and humans (1).

Etiology

The most frequently isolated etiological agents according to (1):

  • Trichophyton verrucosum, in 98% of cases.
  • T. mentagrophytes, T. canis, T. rubrum in a lower percentage.
  • T. megnii, T. quinckeanum

According to (21) the main cause of bovine trichophytosis is T. mentagrophytes. However (20) states that the causes isolated as producers of Trichophytosis are: M. gypseum, T. mentagrophytes, T. rubrum and T. simii.

T. verrucosum is highly adapted to bovines and their way of life; the rest of the animals are infected by this species (1).

Epidemiology

Among the affected species: sheep, pigs, horses and humans (1); (19) lists goats as a susceptible species.

The epidemiological data to highlight according to (1) are:

  • Disease of worldwide distribution.
  • Transmission via spores and infected cutaneous material, either direct or indirect; in the latter, insects may act as vectors. The presentation of the disease is also linked to the existence of healthy carriers (although this has not been demonstrated, as no cases of subclinical or latent infections are known).
  • The spread of the disease within a group is favored by: warm and humid climates, poor hygiene, etc. Microtraumas act as predisposing factors in the onset of disease.
  • During the disease, immunity develops in the host organism, demonstrated by agglutination techniques; this explains why the disease normally presents in young animals. This immunity may show cross-reactivity with other species of cutaneous fungi.

Clinical signs

The incubation period is 2 or 3 weeks; lentil-sized nodules appear under the coat and the hair stands erect in the affected area. As the disease progresses, areas of hair loss appear, subsequently covered by crusts along with inflamed areas prone to bleeding. Pruritus is generally absent (1).

With this clinical picture, the animal tends toward lower production, reduced weight gain, hide damage, obstruction of commercial transit, etc., causing significant economic losses in the farming operation (1).

Lesions

Macroscopic and histological findings (1):

  • Histologically, an intense cellular reaction is observed in the affected skin areas.
  • The process is initially limited to the superficial skin layers, while in advanced stages of the disease it parasitizes exclusively the hair and hair follicles.
  • Hyperkeratosis and parakeratosis occur, with hair loss resulting in elimination of the fungus, beginning at the center of the affected cutaneous area.

Diagnosis

If possible, examine the patient in a dark place using a Wood’s lamp to try to observe fluorescence. If fluorescence is present, take some fluorescent hairs with tweezers for microscopic examination (5).

Skin scales or hair are placed on a slide with a 10% NaOH solution, covered, and the presence of arthrospores is sought under the microscope. The preparation should be heated for 10 minutes before observation (5).

Incubation in Sabouraud with cycloheximide and chloramphenicol is required for 3–6 weeks according to (1); according to (5) it cannot be ruled out until incubation for one month has elapsed.

If a fungus appears, examine the colonial appearance, texture, pigment, etc. Then examine the mycelium and other structures microscopically with cotton blue staining, attempting to demonstrate the main characteristics of dermatophytes already mentioned in the introduction (5).

Treatment

The treatment recommended in (1) is:

  • Iodine tincture
  • Mustard oil derivatives
  • Chloramine
  • Peracetic acid
  • Griseofulvin as medicated feed, 0.5 g/kg body weight; animals that recover receive it for two more days; it should not be administered to lactating cows since it is excreted in milk.
  • Vitamin A.

(10) has tested a treatment based on different doses of thiabendazole for 10 days and has observed that animals recover at the same time regardless of the dose tested.

Control

  • Treatment of affected and healthy animals with Griseofulvin.
  • Clean and disinfect housing with 5–10% formalin.
  • Quarantine (1).

(11) has tested immunization of a herd whose affection by this pathology in 1979 was 70%; after an immunoprophylactic period, affection in 1987 was reduced to 0%.

PIG DERMATOMYCOSIS

Rare in presentation compared to those of other domestic animals. The most frequent etiologies according to (1):

  • Microsporum nanum — soil dermatophyte
  • Trichophyton mentagrophytes — obligate parasite

Epidemiology

(1) highlights the following epidemiological data:

  • Sources of infection: various animals and utensils.
  • Once the first case appears, it spreads throughout the herd by both direct and indirect contact. Passage through animals produces an enhancement in the virulence of the causative agent.
  • Regarding the development of immunity, this is not well studied.

Clinical signs

Discrete pruritus in the affected area; hair loss is rare since it is rarely affected. Greyish-greenish crusts. The process resolves approximately 4–8 weeks after the first symptoms are observed, by spontaneous recovery (1).

Diagnosis

As above.

Treatment

Similar to bovines.

DERMATOMYCOSIS IN CARNIVORES

Notable is microsporosis caused by Microsporum canis (transmissible to humans) (1).

Other Microsporum species frequently involved in the process cited by (1):

  • M. audouinii
  • M. gypseum

Microsporum is characterized by cigar-shaped conidia.

Epidemiology

Transmission by direct or indirect contact between animals (1).

M. canis is transmitted to humans causing tinea capitis (7, 24).

Crowding of animals greatly influences disease presentation, as do: lice, fleas, flies, mites, etc., which act as vectors (1).

Young and weakened animals are particularly susceptible (1).

Inapparent clinical infection may exist, especially in cats; this is important in producing clinical disease in dogs with which they cohabit (1).

Clinical signs

Depilated areas, hair that detaches easily, mainly affecting the head and extremities. Spontaneous recovery only occurs after a long period of suffering from the disease (1).

According to bibliographic references (13), dermatophytosis has been observed in sheep in which the etiological agent was M. canis. The clinical description is: alopecic areas of 1–2 cm diameter, circular and normally located in the flank area.

Diagnosis

Similar to the above.

Treatment

Oral administration of griseofulvin with topical antifungal treatment (once the areas are clipped for better penetration). Generally, improvement is noted within 2–4 weeks (1).

Griseofulvin doses:

  • 25–50 mg/kg body weight — dog
  • 40–60 mg/kg body weight — cat
  • Daily for 3–5 weeks.

When possible, animals should be isolated; good results are obtained with an ointment composed of salicylic acid and benzoic acid, which has keratolytic action (3).

Aspergillosis

Affects the respiratory system, gestating uterus and rarely the digestive system.

Etiology

Main Aspergillus species causing this pathology: A. niger, A. flavus, A. fumigatus.

Epidemiology

This disease is distributed worldwide.

Clinical signs

Pulmonary aspergillosis: dyspnoea, cough, weight loss. Nodules similar to those found in tuberculosis (forming cavities). Histopathologically they present the structure of a granuloma. Pneumonias caused by Aspergillus spp. are cited in goats.

Mycotic abortion: abortion, foetal membranes of dirty greyish-yellow color. Produced by haematogenous spread following pulmonary infection. Mycotic abortions caused by A. fumigatus in goats are cited in the literature.

Diagnosis

Direct examination of tissue or deep scrapings in 10% sodium hydroxide. Small parts of thick septate hyphae are characteristic. Typical conidial heads are only observed in lungs where oxygen access exists.

Aspergillus grows rapidly on blood agar and Sabouraud agar at room temperature or in an incubator. Colonies are initially white, then change to light or dark green; they are velvety and flat.

The genus is identified by the presence of conidiophores with large terminal vesicles bearing sterigmata from which chains of spores or conidia are produced.

AVIAN ASPERGILLOSIS

Clinically characterized by producing pneumomycosis, accompanied by lesions in air sacs and serous membranes.

Etiology

Aspergillus fumigatus and other species.

Epidemiology

The species mainly affected by this pathology are: chickens, ducks, geese and turkeys; other birds are also affected (parrots, cockatoos, raptors, etc.).

Infection through the eggshell is considered a possibility. Particularly susceptible are animals with vitamin deficiency, newborns and immunosuppressed individuals; from 14 days of age, resistance to this disease increases. It can penetrate the egg and infect embryos. Mortality can reach 100%. Triggering factors influence disease presentation.

Clinical signs

Laboured breathing, rattling, gasping, weight loss and somnolence. The tongue and palate may have a greenish, crusted and suppurated appearance.

Inoculation of adult chickens with A. fumigatus has shown: pneumonia, pulmonary hypertension, ventricular hypertrophy and dilation, right valvular insufficiency, portal hypertension and ascites. Occasional nervous manifestations occur.

Pathological anatomy

Inflamed mucosae will show a dirty greenish-yellow coating, with yellowish nodules. Lung with bronchopneumonia foci of different sizes. Mycotic granuloma.

Diagnosis

Similar to the above.

Treatment

  • Flavofungin, moronal, trichomat.
  • Avoid predisposing factors.
  • Good ventilation.
  • Reduce humidity.
