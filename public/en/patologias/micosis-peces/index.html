Main Fungal Diseases in Fish

MAIN DISEASES CAUSED BY CHROMISTS AND FUNGI (EUMYCOTA) IN FISH

Saprolegniasis — Ulcerative mycosis or cotton mouth disease

Introduction

Mycotic infection of the skin and gills, caused by fungi belonging to the order Saprolegniales. However, from similar lesions, fungi belonging to other orders may also be isolated, such as: Pytium, Leptomitus, etc.

Aetiology

Saprolegniaceous fungi are aquatic moulds belonging to the class Oomycetes.

CLASS OOMYCETES, KINGDOM CHROMISTA

  • SIX ORDERS: among which is the Saprolegniales
  • Family: Saprolegniaceae

They are found abundantly in clear waters and are easily isolated. However, many species are soil-dwelling. Most species in this order are saprophytic and have little direct economic importance. Nevertheless, a few species are significant parasites.

They are morphologically characterised by having non-septate (coenocytic) somatic hyphae and septate reproductive hyphae. Asexual reproduction occurs by biflagellate zoospores and sexual reproduction by monoflagellate zoospores. Macroscopically they resemble a cottony mass in the water.

The classification of Oomycetes is carried out using keys based on the morphological characteristics of the sexual structures (oogonium and antheridium). (Fig. 24-5)

Culture

They are among the easiest fungi to isolate and cultivate in the laboratory. For sampling, a sterile container is filled halfway with the water to be analysed and bait is added in the form of crushed hemp or sesame seeds. Within a few days, colonies appear that can be transferred to Petri dishes containing 25 ml of sterile distilled water.

Nutritional requirements: Glucose appears to be the best carbohydrate source for most species, although different species are capable of using other carbohydrates: mannose, sucrose, etc. In general they are unable to utilise nitrates, but grow well in media containing organic nitrogen in the form of peptone or amino acids. Most species are able to synthesise the vitamins needed for their growth.

Inorganic requirements: Mg, Ca, Zn, Mn, Fe and S. Sulphate cannot be utilised, but sulphur can be adequately supplied in organic form as cysteine, cystine, glutathione or methionine. The optimal pH range for growth is between 4–6.

Epidemiology

Members of the Family Saprolegniaceae are among the most frequent aquatic fungi; they are present in most freshwater environments. Some species, capable of tolerating a certain degree of salinity, live even in brackish estuarine waters when the salinity does not exceed 2.8%. Higher salinity limits the distribution of these fungi. Saprolegniaceous fungi are abundant in moist soils. Although they have not been observed in all parts of the world, they almost certainly have a cosmopolitan distribution.

Some species of the genus Saprolegnia, such as Saprolegnia parasitica, cause disease in fish and their eggs, and can cause significant damage in hatcheries or fish farms.

Saprolegniasis is usually a secondary disease associated with predisposing factors such as: poor management, overcrowding conditions, environmental contamination.

Other predisposing factors:

  • Bacterial or viral diseases.
  • Temperature. Most epidemics arise when temperatures are low, and can be controlled by raising the temperature. However, there is some controversy, as some authors believe that a rise in temperature leads to stress in the fish which would result in a mycotic superinfection (secondary infections following trauma usually occur at any temperature).
  • Certain physiological moments of the fish (spawning period, during which mucus secretion is altered).

Pathogenesis

Once the fungus invades the skin, it establishes focally, invading the spongy layer of the dermis and spreading laterally over the epidermis; this leads to an imbalance in body fluids and peripheral circulatory failure (shock) due to inability to maintain circulating blood volume.

Normally the disease follows a chronic course and bacterial complications may arise. Infection in salmonids usually follows a more acute course and can cause death within thirty days.

In contrast to typical saprolegniosis, ulcerative mycosis is much more invasive, producing small skin ulcers (5 mm) that enlarge to reach 25 mm in diameter. Muscular necrosis occurs and internal organs may frequently become affected. From this clinical form, species of Aphanomyces are usually isolated, though in some cases species of Saprolegnia have also been isolated.

Lesions

Characterised by the appearance of white-grey patches on the skin and gills of the fish; underwater they have a cottony appearance, initially circular; in the final stage the patches become dark, growing radially to form a raised periphery. There are some cases of infections affecting internal organs arising from prior epidermal lesions (intestine, peritoneum) in which ulcers are observed.

Diagnosis

Histopathological: in H&E-stained skin sections, branched, non-septate hyphae of approximately 20 microns in diameter can be observed; below this mycelium zone, necrosis and superficial dermal oedema are seen, as well as deep myofibrillar necrosis and extensive haemorrhage. Other methods to demonstrate hyphae: PAS, Grocott technique (silver impregnation).

Microbiological: as described in the aetiology section, by culture and light microscopy.

Scanning Electron Microscopy: either observing the fungus directly in sections of affected tissues, or sections of the fungus grown in culture medium.

Control

Prophylaxis:

  • Maintain adequate rearing conditions.
  • Correct feeding.
  • Avoid overcrowding.
  • Maintain good water quality.

Systemic mycoses

Systemic mycosis of catfish: characterised by the appearance of skin ulcers, abscesses and peritonitis. Possibility of haematogenous dissemination. Caused by Dematiaceous fungi, Moniliales.

Cerebral mycetoma: caused by Exophiala salmonis. Affects trout, producing granulomas in which hyphae can be observed.

Scolecobasidium humicola infections: in salmon, skin lesions, abdominal distension.

Sphaeropsidales (Phoma herbarum) infections: in salmonids, anal swelling and haemorrhages on skin and fins, irregular swimming.

Ichthyosporidium (Ichthyophonus hoferi) infections: systemic granulomas in many fish species, sandpaper-skin effect (dermal granulomas).

Dermocystidium marinum infections: cysts in gills, fin and skin damage, affects salmonids.

Prymnesium parvum infections: disease caused by the toxin of this organism; fish leap out of shallow water; the toxin increases gill permeability leading to osmotic imbalance.

Branchiomyces spp. infections: also known as gill rot; gills showing ischaemic necrosis due to intravascular growth of the fungus.

Hyphomycosis: disease affecting various fish species, caused by Hyphomycetes (Aspergillus spp., Penicillium spp., Acremonium spp., etc.). Clinically results in lethargy, disorientation, wasting and small ulcerative skin lesions, mainly located on the dorsal and ventral head region and along the lateral line.

Crustacean pathologies

Fusarium solani in Litopenaeus vannamei (White shrimp)

The main aetiological agent causing this mycotic infection by imperfect fungi belongs to the genus Fusarium, principally Fusarium solani. This fungal species infects juveniles and adults of penaeid shrimp and its distribution is worldwide. They are naturally present in soil and detritus, acting as an opportunistic pathogen affecting wounded or stressed organisms. They interfere with gas exchange, locomotion, feeding and reproduction.

Diagnosis. During shrimp grow-out, in fungal diseases caused by Fusarium, wet mount analysis or examination of histological sections to observe hyphae and the typical canoe-shaped macroconidia characteristic of the reproductive structures of these fungi is also useful. Culture media for fungi, such as Sabouraud dextrose, are also recommended.

Clinical signs. In juveniles and adults, marked inflammation and melanisation caused by Fusarium solani are observed; the most obvious lesions occur in the appendages, especially at the bases of the pereiopods and gills. Lesions associated with cuticle abrasions or wounds are also common, since the fungus lacks the ability to penetrate intact cuticle. Its lesions manifest as melanisation of the gills (black gill disease), at the base of the appendages and on the cuticle.

Treatment. The use of Trifluralin (Treflan®) at a concentration of 10 μg/L (0.01 parts per million, ppm) in the culture water is recommended as a preventive measure to inactivate zoospores before introducing larvae. As a treatment, a concentration of 100 μg/L is recommended (Anderson, 1990). Because this compound is unstable in water, it must be applied periodically (every 8 hours) to maintain the therapeutic dose. Trifluralin has a half-life of between 30 and 138 minutes in seawater (Williams et al., 1986), so its environmental impact is very low. The timely application of this medication must be emphasised, before the mycosis becomes generalised in the population, since once the fungus is established in larval tissue, it cannot be inhibited by Trifluralin. Although reported to be less effective than Trifluralin, malachite green oxalate at a concentration of 6–10 ng/L is also recommended as a prophylactic against larval mycosis. It is essential to take strict sanitary measures and disinfect equipment and facilities, ensuring the removal of detritus and disinfection of surfaces. Some disinfectants used include calcium hypochlorite at 500 ppm or more, formalin at 50 ppm or benzalkonium chloride at 50 ppm.

For infections caused by Fusarium, no chemical treatment has proven effective; but bearing in mind that it is a saprophytic fungus that develops in decomposing organic matter, appropriate management measures should be emphasised to minimise unfavourable bottom and water column conditions, meticulous hygiene in facilities, and timely removal of infected animals.

Aphanomycosis

Commonly known as crayfish plague, it virtually destroyed the populations of native crayfish in the rivers and lakes of Germany in the last century. Around 1870 the disease spread from France to Germany (1878–82) moving eastward; between 1891–96 it had decimated the crayfish of Russia and crossed the Urals into western Siberia. In 1894 it was introduced into Lithuania. Scandinavia, which had remained free for a long time, was invaded by aphanomycosis in 1929.

Better known as crayfish plague, it has contributed to the near extinction of the native river crayfish (Austropotamobius pallipes) on the Iberian Peninsula. Aphanomycosis is caused by the fungus Aphanomyces astaci, endemic to American crayfish which are, for the most part, immune to its effects. The continued spread of aphanomycosis throughout Spain is associated with the expansion of the red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii) and the signal crayfish (Pacifastacus leniusculus). The native crayfish is now a threatened and globally endangered species.

Aetiology

For a long time, the aetiological agent remained unclear. Hofer believed it to be Bacterium pestis astaci, until Schikora (1903) demonstrated that the phycomycete Aphanomyces astaci Schikora is the causative agent. In 1935 Schaperclaus provided definitive proof that Aphanomyces causes the disease. Nybelin (1935) and Mannsfeld (1942) confirmed these results.

Biology and habitat

This fungus lacks sexual reproduction and its survival depends exclusively on the production of asexual zoospores and the presence of river crayfish. The designation of waters as “chronically infected” by this fungus is incorrect, since A. astaci is only found chronically in carrier crayfish species, being unable to survive outside river crayfish (Cerenius et al., 1987). As European species succumb at a rate of 100%, once the crayfish die, the plague disappears with them — a fact corroborated by field experiments (Cerenius & Söderhäll, 1992).

Aphanomyces carries its name for good reason, because it is very difficult to see. The hyphae penetrate and destroy the endocuticle of the chitinous carapace, which softens and becomes brittle at points of greatest destruction. It also attacks connective tissue and, above all, the nervous system; exceptionally it invades muscles. Nerve lesions are probably the decisive cause of death, even though lesions of the carapace predispose to bacterial infections, which may also play a non-negligible role in the fatal outcome of aphanomycosis.

Clinical signs and symptoms

  • Symptoms are not always immediately apparent.
  • As the disease progresses, crayfish show certain symptoms.
  • Crayfish often adopt a supine position and move their legs incessantly.
  • The fungus attacks and destroys the articular membranes, causing the death of the crayfish.
  • Some legs, and often the claws, detach.
  • The anal region is another preferred site of attack by the fungus.

Treatment, prophylaxis and hygiene

Lethargic and dead crayfish must be removed from the water and buried. Where feasible, animals used for restocking waters should be kept under observation and quarantine.

Importance of aphanomycosis

  • Disease introduced in the 1970s; responsible for the disappearance of the native crayfish.
  • The red swamp crayfish, Procambarus clarkii, and the signal crayfish, Pacifastacus leniusculus, are carriers of aphanomycosis and transmit the disease to native crayfish.
  • Carrier species, while highly resistant to the disease, can contract and die from it when their immune system is compromised by stress or other infections.

Aphanomyces astaci

It is a strict fungus whose mycelium can only survive in river crayfish (Cerenius and Söderhäll, 1992). It is known that the survival capacity of spores is limited, 2–3 days, and that it does not produce resting spores.

Pathobiology: Infection begins on the non-sclerotised cuticle. Zoospores germinate and produce a mycelium that rapidly grows inside the cuticle, reaching the internal body cavity.

Mechanism of introduction

This fungus is endemic to American crayfish, most of which are immune to its effects, in contrast to European species which are highly susceptible (Gutierrez et al., 1997). It causes aphanomycosis or crayfish plague, a disease responsible for the severe decline of the native crayfish.

The distribution of the native crayfish has been reduced to the upper reaches of rivers, even to altitudes where it had not previously been found.

All populations of introduced crayfish studied in Spain were infected with the fungus, carrying strains specific to their native area (Diéguez-Uribeondo et al., 1995; Diéguez-Uribeondo and Temiño, 1998).

Episodes of mass mortality were particularly significant between 1984 and 1986, accounting for almost half (45%) of population losses. Most of these episodes may have been caused by aphanomycosis. Diéguez-Uribeondo et al. (1997a) demonstrated that this disease was responsible for at least nine of thirteen cases studied in natural populations in the last ten years.

The signal crayfish can suffer the disease:

  • In a stress situation, such as intensive farming with high crayfish densities.
  • Through a secondary infection caused by opportunistic bacteria or parasites such as Psorospermium spp., when a decrease in circulating haemocytes occurs.

Ecological impact

Introduced into Europe in 1860 by crayfish of the genus Orconectes limosus (Rafinesque) imported to Italy from North America. The fungus spread throughout the continent, aided by human movements carrying boots and fishing equipment contaminated with spores, or by restocking with infected crayfish. Fish also act as passive vectors of the disease. The proliferation of fish farms has contributed to extending the distribution area of the fungus (Gutierrez et al.).

Application of manganese chloride

The application of magnesium chloride has been used experimentally in preventing the spread of the “plague” in aquaculture (Rantamäki et al., 1992). This treatment allows the growth of the fungus to be slowed and the transmission of the disease to be prevented. Although it has not been commercially applied, this treatment may be of great importance for the maintenance of endangered populations, as it allows the still-healthy specimens from affected catchments to be saved. It would also allow the preservation of genetic material at risk of disappearing (Diéguez-Uribeondo, 1998).
