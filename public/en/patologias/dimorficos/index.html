Histoplasmosis

Morphology of H. capsulatum (5)

Etiological agent: Histoplasma capsulatum, which has affinity for the reticuloendothelial system. Clinically: lymph node inflammation, diarrhea, fever, cough, ascites, cachexia and death (1).

Direct examination: H. capsulatum is small and rarely found extracellularly; it is genuinely difficult to demonstrate in clinical materials. It stains with Giemsa and Wright methods; intracellular organisms appear as small cells (4).

EPIZOOTIC LYMPHANGITIS OR FALSE GLANDERS

Etiological agent: H. farciminosum. Affects solipeds. Characterized by the appearance of nodules in the vascular walls and thrombosis in the lymphatic vessels. Cutaneous forms exist that ulcerate. String-of-beads appearance in affected lymphatic vessels (1).

Sporotrichosis

Ericka Himelda Jordan Padilla. Veterinary Surgeon

Dermatology of Small Species. Private clinic and teaching at the UNIVER of Guadalajara, Jalisco, Mexico.

Sporotrichosis is an infection caused by the dimorphic fungus Sporothrix schenckii, of subacute or chronic course. It primarily affects the skin and lymph nodes in the form of gummatous lesions and rarely presents in bones, joints and other organs such as viscera and lungs. (2) It is generally localized in the facial area and extremities. (1)

Sporothrix schenckii is generally found in vegetation, soil and animals. (7)

The first case of Sporotrichosis was reported in 1898 in the USA, by Schenck at the Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore; Erwin F. Smith named the isolated fungus “Sporotrichia”. (1)

Until the early twentieth century, no reports relating to sporotrichosis in animals appear in the literature. In 1907 the first case in animals was described in a rat, and the asteroid body was described. (1) Sporotrichosis in dogs was reported in 1908 and in 1909 in horses and mules. In the 1950s it was reported in camels, domestic poultry and cattle. In the last 20 years, most reports refer to infection in horses, dogs and pigs. (12)

Sporotrichosis is a cosmopolitan disease (5) that develops commonly in warm climates, but has been reported on all continents except the polar circles. (9)

This is not a notifiable disease and therefore no data are available to estimate incidence. (12)

Muñoz and Mayorga at the Dermatological Institute of Jalisco “Dr. José Barba Rubio” in 1992 postulate sporotrichosis as the most frequent subcutaneous mycosis in humans in the state of Jalisco. (15)

In the clinical-epidemiological study on sporotrichosis in the state of Jalisco (1960–1996) carried out by Biol. Jorge Mayorga and Dr. José Barba Rubio, it was observed that the most affected groups are children under 15 years and housewives. (15)

Zoonotic transmission has been confirmed, but has not always been reported; it should be noted that reported cases of transmission are through scratches and/or bites from infected animals. (18) In 1993, a case of zoonotic transmission was confirmed in a veterinarian who was scratched by a cat infected with sporotrichosis. The transmission was confirmed by double isolation from the lesions of the cat and the veterinarian. From these isolates, the 18S RNA fraction of the mycelial phase of the fungus was decoded and determined to be the same causative agent. (16)

Inoculation of the fungus into the host occurs through cutaneous lesions, inhalation and by handling infected dressing material. (8) Infection may be associated with insect bites, handling of fish, birds, dogs, horses, reptiles, rats and armadillos. (8) Most cases are diagnosed by identification of the fungus through culture; however, in a study carried out on mammals at the São Paulo Zoo, Brazil, 30.21% were positive to the intradermal reaction test. (4)

Sporotrichosis is generally acquired through traumatisms with contaminated material (2), rarely through inhalation.

Primary cutaneous sporotrichosis begins through traumatisms with contaminated material; the first lesion presents at the site of fungal entry, producing a sporotrichotic chancre (1, 7). Approximately 10 days later, through interaction of the immune response, the so-called cutaneous-lymphatic complex forms; from this point, the disease tends to follow two courses: involution of lesions and spontaneous recovery (2), or alternatively, spread by continuity, producing very chronic verrucous plaques or gummatous lesions following lymphatic vessels, affecting regional lymphatics and stopping at the major lymphatic vessels (neck, axillae, groins); any state of immunosuppression can cause the disease to disseminate to other organs. (1, 2, 3, 7)

Pulmonary sporotrichosis begins and follows a course similar to that of tuberculosis; first the primary contact occurs, then primary pulmonary sporotrichosis presents as a pneumonic picture, being symptomatic in most cases while remaining limited; systemic dissemination is more difficult from these cases. (2)

Due to its polymorphism, sporotrichosis has many clinical classifications; most agree in dividing clinical aspects according to their immunological relationship.

CLASSIFICATION ACCORDING TO DR. A. GONZÁLEZ OCHOA. (5)

  • LYMPHATIC: Gummatous, ascending from the extremities; gummatous in another region.
  • FIXED TEGUMENTARY: Ulcerative, verrucous, plaque; erythematous-squamous infiltrate.
  • HAEMATOGENOUS: Gummatous disseminated throughout the integument.
  • INTERNAL: Primary pulmonary; secondary in lymphangitic and fixed tegumentary types concurrent with haematogenous-cutaneous type.

CLASSIFICATION PROPOSED BY SAUL IN 1988 (2).

Clinical types:

  • Cutaneous-lymphatic sporotrichosis (70%) normergic, or fixed-cutaneous (25%) hyperergic.
  • Superficial-cutaneous sporotrichosis hypoergic, or cutaneous-haematogenous (2%) anergic.
  • Osteoarticular.
  • Pulmonary and systemic.

CLASSIFICATION OF CLINICAL FORMS IN DOGS (9).

  • CUTANEOUS FORM: Alopecic, circumscribed, plaque-like areas, non-painful.
  • CUTANEOUS-LYMPHATIC FORM: presence of nodules on the skin of the extremities; infection extends to the regional lymph nodes. When nodes rupture, they drain a reddish-brown exudate.
  • DISSEMINATED: Metastatic invasion of tissues outside the area of affected regional lymph nodes.

The causative agent of sporotrichosis is the fungus Sporothrix schenckii (7); its synonyms are S. burmanni, asteroides, indicum and jeanselmi. (5)

Sporothrix schenckii is a dimorphic fungus that within the organism adopts the yeast-like or cigar-body form, and when saprophytic has the form of mycelium. (14)

The parasitic or yeast-like form is obtained by culture on blood agar or brain-heart infusion agar at 37°C and by inoculation of the mycelial phase in susceptible laboratory animals. (5)

Yeasts in tissues are observed as small budding cells or in cigar shape, Gram-positive. The mycelial phase is observed in Sabouraud agar cultures; colonies are cream to black in color, folded, and develop in 3 to 5 days in culture. Under the microscope, simple, ovoid conidia can be observed, born in clusters at the tip of long slender conidiophores, as well as at the sides of thin hyphae. (10)

Mycological diagnosis is the demonstration, isolation and identification of the fungus or its antibodies, and can be carried out using the following laboratory investigation resources:

a) Tissue investigation in infected organic products:

  • Preparations stained by haematoxylin-eosin, Gomori, McManus and PAS techniques. (11)

b) Culture: On Sabouraud agar medium, the presence of typical colonies with clusters of conidia constitutes the diagnosis. It must be converted to the yeast-like morphology during incubation at 37°C on blood agar or brain-heart infusion agar. (10)

c) Cutaneous hypersensitivity: For the intradermal reaction, infiltrates of fungal cultures in liquid media, generally synthetic, consisting of polysaccharides extracted from fungal cells must be used. (3) Two types of sporotrichin exist: from the mycelial phase (M) and from the yeast-like form (L); the difference in use between the two is that the mycelial form will report active disease, and the yeast-like form only reports contact with the fungus. (2) The sporotrichin used for diagnostic purposes consists of glycoprotein fractions extracted from the filamentous phases and is prepared from the polysaccharide of the mycelial phase; in the case of positivity, it provides an inflammatory reaction at 18 and 72 hours, which is a specific diagnostic value. (10)

d) Serological tests:

  • Latex agglutination.
  • Immunodiffusion.
  • Immunoelectrophoresis.
  • Immunoelectrotransfer (Western blot).
  • Direct immunofluorescence.
  • Indirect immunofluorescence. (1, 12, 13, 16, 17)

The techniques mentioned above are only carried out and offered at the Centers for Disease Control, Atlanta, GA. (10)

e) X-rays: Radiographic alterations in lungs, bones and joints are non-specific. (1)

The differential diagnosis of sporotrichosis includes:

  • Tularaemia.
  • Tuberculosis or lymphatic gummatous mycobacteriosis.
  • Verrucous, articular and osseous tuberculosis.
  • Cutaneous-nervous complex in tuberculoid leprosy.
  • Mycetomas.
  • Chromoblastomycosis. (1)
